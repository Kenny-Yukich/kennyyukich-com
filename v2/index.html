The argument

The last mile is about four feet long.

Most AI programs don't fail at the model. They fail about four feet from the person who was supposed to use them.

The demo lands. Leadership is impressed. Licenses get bought. And then the thing sits there, because a tool that saves eleven minutes is worthless to someone who doesn't have a spare eleven minutes to learn it.

I work at a company that builds walking-floor trailers. Most of my colleagues aren't at a desk — they're on a floor, in gloves, in front of a machine that's already running. That constraint turned out to be the most useful design input I've ever had. When your user genuinely cannot be bothered, technically capable stops being a category worth talking about. The only question that survives is whether this gives someone their time back today.

Everything I've published since comes out of answering that question badly a few times before answering it well. The 90-day playbook exists because my first rollout had no baseline and I couldn't prove anything to anyone. The prompt gallery is sorted by department instead of by app because sorting by app is how you build a library nobody opens. The measurement contract exists because someone asked me whether it was working and I didn't have an honest answer.

None of it is proprietary and none of it is theoretical. It ran in a real plant, with real people who had every reason to ignore it. If you're doing the same work somewhere else — a plant, a warehouse, a hospital, a field crew — take any of it. I'd rather you skip the parts I got wrong.

The operating system

Four phases, in this order, for a reason.

PHASE 01

Make the launch measurable before it's visible.

Baseline first. If you can't say what normal looked like before you arrived, every result you report afterward is an opinion, and it will be treated as one.

PHASE 02

Make the first useful result happen on real work.

Not a sandbox, not sample data. Someone's actual backlog, on the day they're actually behind. The first result is the only one that buys you a second conversation.

PHASE 03

Convert early wins into peer-supported habits.

Adoption spreads sideways, not down. Find the behavior in the data, give that person a small visible job, and let the proof come from a colleague instead of from IT.

PHASE 04

Graduate useful behavior into business capability.

A habit that lives in one person's head is a single point of failure. This is where the prompt becomes an agent, and the agent becomes something the department owns.

Running alongside all four: the measurement contract

Same rules every week. Assumptions published where anyone can inspect them. Nothing restated favorably once the numbers are in. It's less exciting than the phases and it's the part that keeps the program alive.

Field notes

Written down while it was still fresh.

Drafts, mostly. I'd rather publish the version with the mistakes still in it than wait for the version that makes me look better.

Draft8 min

The last mile is four feet long

Why the gap that kills AI programs isn't between the model and the workflow — it's between the workflow and a person holding something in both hands. What that constraint does to every design decision downstream.

Draft11 min

Five to sixty-eight: what the number doesn't tell you

The honest version of the headline metric. Which growth was earned, which was a reorg that would have happened anyway, and the two months in the middle where the line went flat and I couldn't explain why.

Draft9 min

What I'd do differently in the first thirty days

I launched before I had a baseline, trained on features instead of on jobs, and let the pilot group self-select into the people who least needed help. Three mistakes, and what each one cost.

Draft5 min

Sort the prompt library by department, not by app

A short craft note. Organizing by tool is how vendors think; organizing by job is how the person searching thinks. The gallery got used roughly four times as much after the re-sort.

Draft7 min

Building an agent for a job that didn't exist yet

I built a personal retrieval agent to get up to speed faster in a manufacturing role. Told as method rather than biography: what I indexed, what I got wrong about chunking, and why the useful part was the questions it forced me to write down.

Draft6 min

Nobody on the floor wants a chatbot

They want an answer without having to interrupt a colleague. Reframing the whole category around that one sentence changed what I built and how I introduced it.
